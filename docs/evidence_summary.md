# FlexTome evidence base: verified literature for a low-cost flexure-pivot rocking microtome

The literature supports three load-bearing claims for the FlexTome paper. First, the pathology access gap in low- and middle-income countries is quantified and large: roughly one pathologist per million people in much of sub-Saharan Africa against one per 15,000–20,000 in the United Kingdom and United States, and 47% of the world's population with little or no access to diagnostics. Second, the mechanics of paraffin sectioning at 3–5 µm are governed by knife geometry (facet ≈35°, clearance 1–10°), by compression that grows steeply below 5–10 µm, and by chatter that arises from insufficient stiffness in the knife–specimen loop, all of which set concrete design targets for a rocking-arm instrument. Third, no prior open-source or 3D-printed sectioning device reaches paraffin thickness: the published devices reach ≥50 µm (vibrating razor), <100 µm (Rapid-Tome), or 250 µm–1 mm (HardwareX slicer), leaving the 3–5 µm paraffin regime unaddressed by low-cost hardware. The flexure-pivot, differential-screw and FDM-materials literatures supply the design equations and property data the engineering analysis needs. Every citation key below corresponds to a row in `references.csv`; facts that could not be confirmed against a retrieved abstract or full text are flagged UNVERIFIED.

## Clinical need and cost context

### A. The histopathology access gap

The pathologist workforce in sub-Saharan Africa is one to two orders of magnitude thinner than in high-income countries. The 2016 survey of African pathology resources reported population per pathologist ranging from 9,264,500 in Niger to 84,133 in Mauritius, against an estimated 15,000–20,000 persons per pathologist in the United Kingdom and United States; Tanzania had 22 pathologists for 48.1 million people, Sierra Leone one for 6.4 million and South Sudan two for 12.2 million ([Nelson 2016](https://doi.org/10.1200/jco.2015.61.9767)) [Nelson2016]. The earlier 2012–13 capacity survey, as cited by later work, found that every sub-Saharan country except Botswana and South Africa had fewer than one pathologist per 500,000 people, and Somalia had none ([Adesina 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/s1470-2045%2812%2970598-3)) [Adesina2013; secondary citation, UNVERIFIED against full text]. The 2018 Lancet Series on pathology and laboratory medicine framed this as a "crucial gap" for universal health coverage and, in its press summary, gave the ratio as roughly one pathologist per million in sub-Saharan Africa, about 50-fold lower than in high-income countries ([Wilson 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0140-6736%2818%2930458-6)) [Wilson2018; paywalled, in-text wording UNVERIFIED]; the Series' roadmap paper is the source secondary reports cite for the estimate that at present training rates it would take on the order of 400 years for the region to reach the US/UK ratio ([Sayed 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0140-6736%2818%2930459-8)) [Sayed2018; UNVERIFIED], with the companion call-to-action arguing that affordable, high-quality pathology is deliverable in LMICs ([Horton 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0140-6736%2818%2930460-4)) [Horton2018]. Even high-income systems are not immune: the number of active US pathologists fell from 15,568 in 2007 to 12,839 in 2017 (−17.5%) while Canada's grew 20.5% ([Metter 2019](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2019.4337)) [Metter2019].

The diagnostics gap is broader than histopathology. The Lancet Commission on diagnostics concluded that 47% of the global population has little or no access to diagnostics; at the primary-care level only about 19% of populations in low- and lower-middle-income countries have access to the simplest tests (other than HIV and malaria), rising to only 60–70% in hospitals, and the undiagnosed fraction for six tracer conditions (35–62%) is the single largest gap in the care cascade ([Fleming 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0140-6736%2821%2900673-5)) [Fleming2021]. Infrastructure cost is part of the barrier: an essential pathology package for a district hospital laboratory was costed at US$150,000–200,000 of equipment versus US$2,000–5,000 for a tier-1 primary facility, with recurrent laboratory costs near 6% of a mid-sized hospital's budget ([Fleming 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcp/aqw143)) [Fleming2017]. A microtome is one of the few pieces of anatomic-pathology hardware with no consumable-free substitute, so its capital cost sits directly on this equipment line.

### B. What commercial manual rotary microtomes cost

Peer-reviewed sources do not list microtome prices, so the figures below come from vendor pages (URLs in `references.csv`, accessed October 2026). New list prices for entry-level manual rotary microtomes are US$22,689.99 for the Leica RM2125 RTS (VWR) [WEB_VWR_RM2125] and US$21,547.60 for the Sakura Accu-Cut SRM 200 (VWR; US$17,100 at GovSci, US$21,575.99 at Grayline) [WEB_VWR_SRM200]; the Thermo/Microm HM 325 is listed at US$8,995 by GMI, with refurbished units at US$7,950 [WEB_GMI_HM325]. Refurbished RM2125 units sell for US$7,950 with warranty and used units for US$2,100–6,999 [WEB_RANKIN_RM2125]. The HardwareX slicer paper independently places basic vibratomes at US$5,000–15,000 and advanced models at US$20,000–50,000 ([Martinez-Martin 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ohx.2024.e00611)) [MartinezMartin2024]. Against this, systematic reviews of open-source scientific hardware find average savings of 87% versus proprietary equivalents, rising to 94% when Arduino electronics and RepRap-class 3D printing are combined ([Pearce 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ohx.2020.e00139)) [Pearce2020], and open hardware is increasingly framed as a structural route to laboratory-equipment access in LMICs ([Wenzel 2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3001931)) [Wenzel2023]. A sub-US$60 bill of materials would therefore represent a >99% reduction relative to new list price and >95% relative to the used market.

## Sectioning mechanics and history

### C. Mechanics of paraffin sectioning

Routine H&E sections are cut at 3–5 µm; this is stated in every histotechnology text and vendor guide but has no primary paper, so it is cited here from the Leica microtomy guide [WEB_LEICA_MICROTOMY] and marked as practice rather than a measured result. Section thickness as delivered deviates substantially from the dial setting even on commercial rotary microtomes: direct measurement at 154 sites found more than two-thirds of paraffin sections outside ±10% of the nominal value, and a CNN trained on matched images inferred thickness with a mean absolute error of 0.28 µm ([Fujisawa 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.labinv.2026.106166)) [Fujisawa2026]. This is a useful calibration target: a FlexTome validation that measures section thickness directly, rather than trusting the feed, would be following current best practice.

Compression is the dominant artefact and it grows sharply as sections get thinner. On the rotary microtome, thick sections are commonly compressed 15% or more, and below 5–10 µm compression may exceed 50% ([Dempster 1943](https://doi.org/10.3109/10520294309105785)) [Dempster1943]; the same author's earlier papers laid out the geometric mechanics of the knife (facet, clearance, rake) and the distortions introduced by the sliding microtome ([Dempster 1942a](https://doi.org/10.1002/ar.1090840303); [Dempster 1942b](https://doi.org/10.1002/ar.1090840304)) [Dempster1942a; Dempster1942b; details UNVERIFIED]. Knife geometry matters at exactly the FlexTome operating point: knives with the usual ~31° facet bevel produced gross compression at 4 µm or thinner, whereas a 21° bevel gave a superior 4 µm ribbon with little folding and no change in cell morphology ([Marengo 1967](https://doi.org/10.3109/10520296709114978)) [Marengo1967]. The same physics is better quantified in ultramicrotomy, where conventional sectioning with a 45° knife compresses sections by 20–35% of their height and lateral oscillation of the knife minimises it ([Studer 2000](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2818.2000.00638.x)) [Studer2000], and where compression, crevassing and knife marks have been mapped against sectioning angle in vitreous sections ([Al-Amoudi 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsb.2005.01.003)) [AlAmoudi2005; values UNVERIFIED].

Clearance and facet angles are set by convention. Disposable blades and knives have a facet angle of approximately 35°, and Leica recommends a clearance of 1–5° for its holders [WEB_LEICA_MICROTOMY]; other guides give 5–10° for disposable blades, narrowing to 3–5° for hard tissue to suppress vibration and widening to 7–10° for soft tissue to limit compression, with bevel + clearance + rake summing to 90° [WEB_MERCEDES_TROUBLESHOOT]. These are practitioner recommendations, not measurements, and the paper should present them as such.

Cutting force in paraffin microtomy has essentially not been measured in the open literature. The only instrumented histological microtome found senses knife load during the stroke and shows that force variation tracks specimen texture, but its abstract reports no absolute values ([Willis 1995](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2818.1995.tb03581.x)) [Willis1995; force magnitudes UNVERIFIED]. The relevant mechanics therefore come from the general cutting literature: fracture-mechanics analysis of "pressing and slicing" shows that adding a tangential (slicing) component to blade motion lowers the normal force required to cut thin floppy slices ([Atkins 2004](https://doi.org/10.1023/b:jmsc.0000021451.17182.86)) [Atkins2004], and experiments on soft solids show that shear strongly reduces the force to initiate and propagate a cut ([Reyssat 2012](https://doi.org/10.1103/physrevlett.109.244301)) [Reyssat2012]. For a rocking arm, whose specimen trajectory is an arc, this argues that a small slicing component is not a defect but may help; it also means a FlexTome cutting-force measurement would be a genuine contribution.

Chatter, alternating thick and thin sections or washboard marks, is a stiffness and vibration phenomenon. It was described in paraffin sections in the pathology literature of the 1950s ([Bayley 1955](https://doi.org/10.1002/path.1700700236)) [Bayley1955], and knife chatter has been shown to generate periodic artefacts that mimic real structure ([Warshawsky 1983](https://doi.org/10.1002/ar.1092070315)) [Warshawsky1983]. Practitioner guides attribute alternating thick–thin sections to loose clamping, dull edges and clearance mis-set [WEB_MERCEDES_TROUBLESHOOT], which in engineering terms is the compliance of the knife–holder–specimen loop. The design implication for FlexTome is that stiffness of the blade stage and arm pivot, not feed resolution, is the limiting quantity for section-to-section uniformity, and a measured loop stiffness and first natural frequency should be reported.

### D. The Cambridge rocking microtome and its displacement

The ribbon-cutting automatic microtome was invented in Cambridge in 1882–84 by Caldwell and Threlfall; the hand-worked machine delivered a continuous band of accurately cut sections at about 100 per minute ([Caldwell 1884](https://doi.org/10.1242/jcs.s2-24.96.648); [Threlfall 1930](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-185x.1930.tb00903.x)) [Caldwell1884; Threlfall1930]. Horace Darwin's rocking microtome of 1885 simplified this: the razor is fixed and the specimen, carried on a pivoted arm, passes across it in an arc in a rocking motion; a surviving early example shows the arm resting on a threaded steel shaft advanced by a cog wheel driven from the operating lever, with two return springs and a string-and-pulley drive [WEB_SCIENCEMUSEUM_ROCKER; WEB_WHIPPLE_0683]. Its simplicity allowed it to be produced at a fraction of competitors' prices, it was first marketed in 1885, and the design remained essentially unchanged until the 1970s [WEB_CUDL_CSIC; WEB_RCPATH_ROCKER]. It was capable of very thin work: a modified Cambridge rocker was used to cut sections for electron microscopy ([Eaves 1954](https://doi.org/10.1016/0014-4827%2854%2990157-3)) [Eaves1954]. The rotary archetype that displaced it is Minot's, in which the specimen moves vertically on a slide past a fixed knife with automatic screw feed; Minot described two forms in 1897 ([Minot 1897](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.5.127.857)) [Minot1897]. Why the rotary won is not established by any primary paper found here; the standard history is Bracegirdle's monograph, whose sources survey is cited ([Bracegirdle 1977](https://doi.org/10.1177/007327537701500201)) [Bracegirdle1977]. The commonly given reasons, the rocker cuts a slightly curved (cylindrical) section face, has a limited block size, and the rotary's planar stroke and heavier casting suit large clinical blocks, should be stated as received wisdom, not as a cited finding (UNVERIFIED).

The FlexTome inverts the rocker's one acknowledged weakness in a way worth stating explicitly: the parasitic curvature of the arc is a function of arm length and stroke angle, and the flexure-pivot centre shift (Section F) is a second small correction on top of it; both scale predictably and can be bounded in the analysis.

### E. Prior low-cost and 3D-printed sectioning hardware

No published low-cost or 3D-printed device sections at paraffin thickness. The Rapid-Tome is a 3D-printed plant microtome (≈18 h print, five hardware-store metal parts) that produces hand-fed sections of fresh or woody stems thinner than 100 µm; it has no micrometer feed and no paraffin capability, and its cost is given only as "a fraction" of a commercial microtome ([Thomas 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13007-023-00986-3)) [Thomas2023]. The HardwareX tissue precision slicer costs about US$853 in parts, is printed in autoclavable Nylon-12, cuts with a razor blade, and covers 250 µm to 1 mm slices (claiming 0.25 µm stage positioning), for live organoid work rather than histology ([Martinez-Martin 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ohx.2024.e00611)) [MartinezMartin2024]. Older improvised instruments occupy the same thickness regime: a vibrating-razor sectioner built from a hair clipper and a nut-and-bolt advance for under US$50 cut fixed tissue uniformly at 50 µm or more ([Bazer 1980](https://doi.org/10.3109/10520298009067894)) [Bazer1980], and a hand microtome made from a brass tube and a micrometer spindle serves botanical razor sectioning ([Turner 2024](https://doi.org/10.3120/0024-9637-70.2.97)) [Turner2024]. Title-constrained searches of OpenAlex for "3D printed cryostat", "open-source vibratome" and "low-cost microtome" returned no peer-reviewed designs; any such projects exist only as grey literature (UNVERIFIED).

The imaging side of a low-cost histology chain is better served. The OpenFlexure microscope is an open-source, 3D-printed, motorised laboratory microscope of which more than 100 units have been produced locally in Tanzania and Kenya for educational, research and clinical use ([Collins 2020](https://doi.org/10.1364/boe.385729)) [Collins2020]; its one-piece printed flexure stage achieves sub-micron motion over 8 × 8 × 4 mm with drift below 20 µm per week without temperature control ([Sharkey 2016](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.4941068)) [Sharkey2016]. That result is also the strongest available evidence that FDM-printed compliant mechanisms can deliver micron-scale repeatability, which is the premise of the FlexTome blade stage.

## Engineering foundations

### F. Cross-strip flexure pivot theory and flexure fatigue

The crossed-strip (cross-spring) pivot has a closed-form small-deflection theory. Wittrick derived the stiffness and centre-shift of the symmetrical crossed flexure pivot ([Wittrick 1948](https://doi.org/10.1071/ch9480121c)) [Wittrick1948] and then showed how both depend on where the strips cross: the second-order centre shift vanishes when the strips cross at about 12.73% of their length from the moving end (λ ≈ 0.1273), at the cost of higher stiffness ([Wittrick 1951](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0001925900000470)) [Wittrick1951]. Young's experimental investigation gave the first rule-of-thumb formulas for spring constant, bending stress and pivot-point motion ([Young 1944](https://doi.org/10.1115/1.4009358)) [Young1944], and Haringx's elliptic-integral treatment extended the analysis to large deflections and established that the parasitic shift is directed at phase φ = θ/2 relative to the rotation ([Haringx 1949](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf02120338)) [Haringx1949]. For the stiffness formula itself, linear beam theory (clamped–clamped strip, one end rotating about the crossing point) gives, per strip, K = (EI/L)[1 + 3(1 − 2λ)²], so that two strips crossing at their midpoints (λ = 0.5) give K = 2EI/L and strips meeting at their ends (λ = 0) give K = 8EI/L; these limiting values are the ones quoted in the design literature, but the general expression was derived here and should be checked against Wittrick's text before being quoted as his (UNVERIFIED coefficient). The centre shift is second order in rotation, d ≈ c(λ)·L·θ², with the components normalised by strip length in Wittrick's equations ([Thalmann 2022](https://doi.org/10.1115/1.4053471)) [Thalmann2022]; for the generalized pivot the component along the symmetry axis is second order and the transverse component third order in θ, and the shift can be minimised by placing the crossing point appropriately, with cartwheel pivots offering roughly five-fold smaller shift than a standard cross-spring pivot ([Zhao & Bi 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mechmachtheory.2010.05.004)) [ZhaoBi2010]. Non-dimensional stiffness and stress models for arbitrary crossing position are given by the same group ([Zhao & Bi 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mechmachtheory.2009.10.001)) [ZhaoBi2009], and a pseudo-rigid-body four-bar model gives closed-form estimates validated against FEA ([Jensen 2002](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0094-114x%2802%2900007-1)) [Jensen2002]. Nonlinear FEA confirms the optimum crossing ratio at λ = 0.127–0.133 and shows that compound "butterfly" arrangements reduce parasitic shift up to eight-fold ([Marković 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/15397734.2016.1231614)) [Markovic2016]; experimental characterisation under lateral load shows that both stiffness and shift depend on the applied load, which matters for a pivot carrying cutting force ([Zelenika 2002](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0141-6359%2802%2900149-6)) [Zelenika2002]. Benchmarks for what refined pivots achieve include axis drift below 0.3 µm over ±10° for a triple-cross-spring design ([Liu 2014](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.4897271)) [Liu2014]. Textbook treatment of leaf, cross-strip and parallelogram flexures, including the parallelogram stage FlexTome uses for the blade carrier, is in Smith's monograph ([Smith 2000](https://doi.org/10.1201/9781482282962)) [Smith2000].

For FlexTome the practical consequences are: (i) with feeler-gauge blades crossing at the midpoint, the stiffness is the minimum 2EI/L and the centre shift is of order 0.1 L θ² (coefficient to be computed, not quoted); for a 25 mm strip and ±10° stroke this is tens of micrometres, far larger than a 5 µm section but systematic and in-plane, so it changes the cutting arc rather than the thickness, and (ii) the Wittrick λ ≈ 0.127 geometry is available if the analysis shows the shift matters.

Fatigue of thin steel flexures is well characterised. Tests on 66 wire-EDM steel and bronze flexure hinges with 50 µm sections showed that admissible stresses for low-roughness flexures (Ra 0.2 µm) are at least as high as for standard fatigue specimens, so bulk spring-steel S–N data can be used for thin blades ([Henein 1999](https://doi.org/10.1117/12.357815)) [Henein1999]; leaf-type crossed pivots have been shape-optimised to minimise peak stress for aerospace fatigue life ([Freire Gómez 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.precisioneng.2015.03.003)) [FreireGomez2015]. Feeler-gauge stock is hardened carbon spring steel; its endurance limit is not reported in these papers and should be taken from the stock datasheet (UNVERIFIED here). Additive manufacture of compliant mechanisms is established in metals ([Merriam 2013](https://doi.org/10.5194/ms-4-381-2013)) [Merriam2013] and in printed plastic for the OpenFlexure stage [Sharkey2016]; FlexTome's decision to use steel strips for the pivot and printed plastic only for the clamps and the parallelogram stage is consistent with the creep and fatigue limitations of PLA/PETG summarised in Section H.

### G. Differential screws and ratchet indexing

The differential screw is a textbook fine-adjustment element: two threads of different pitch on one spindle, one engaging the frame and the other the carriage, advance the carriage per revolution by the pitch difference, so M8 × 1.25 against M8 × 1.0 gives 0.25 mm/rev, and a 50-tooth ratchet on that spindle yields 5 µm per click; the principle, its backlash and friction behaviour, and its use with flexure guides are treated in the ultra-precision design text ([Smith & Chetwynd 2017](https://doi.org/10.1201/9781315272603)) [SmithChetwynd2017]. OpenAlex title searches for "differential screw" returned no dedicated peer-reviewed design paper (results were dominated by unrelated uses of the phrase), so the equations should be presented as standard and cited to the textbook. Ratchet-and-pawl indexing of a feed screw is the mechanism Darwin's rocker itself used (a cog wheel at the base of the threaded shaft advanced by the operating lever) [WEB_WHIPPLE_0683]; no peer-reviewed analysis of ratchet indexing resolution for microtome feed was found, and the FlexTome analysis of pawl engagement error and screw lead error will stand on its own (literature gap, UNVERIFIED).

### H. FDM part properties: PLA and PETG

Baseline tensile data for desktop-FDM PLA are consistent across studies. Parts from open-source RepRap printers averaged 56.6 MPa tensile strength and 3368 MPa elastic modulus for PLA (28.5 MPa and 1807 MPa for ABS) ([Tymrak 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.matdes.2014.02.038)) [Tymrak2014]; an entry-level printer gave 64 MPa at 45° raster, 58 MPa at 0° and 54 MPa at 90°, with 102 MPa flexural strength ([Letcher 2014](https://doi.org/10.1115/imece2014-39379)) [Letcher2014]. Build orientation, layer thickness and feed rate shift these values, with upright (Z-loaded) parts weakest ([Chacón 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.matdes.2017.03.065)) [Chacon2017; values UNVERIFIED], as reviewed across the FDM parameter literature ([Popescu 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.polymertesting.2018.05.020)) [Popescu2018]. PETG is intermediate between PLA and ABS in strength; both PLA and PETG show a pronounced tension/compression asymmetry and property dependence on print temperature and speed ([Hsueh 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/polym13111758); [Kopar 2023](https://doi.org/10.1515/mt-2023-0202)) [Hsueh2021; Kopar2023]. Bulk-PLA reference values (modulus ≈3–4 GPa, Tg ≈55–60 °C) are collected in the materials review ([Farah 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addr.2016.06.012)) [Farah2016; values from review, not re-verified].

Fatigue and creep are the properties that decide where printed plastic may be used in FlexTome. PLA fatigue life depends on build orientation ([Afrose 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40964-015-0002-3)) [Afrose2016; UNVERIFIED], and a statistical re-analysis of published S–N data provides a reference design curve with an endurance limit extrapolated at 2 × 10⁶ cycles for >95% survival ([Ezeh 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.prostr.2018.06.007)) [Ezeh2018; numeric limit not in abstract]. Tensile creep of FDM PLA (ASTM D2990) is minimised at 0.1 mm layer height and 100% infill ([Waseem 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/polym12122962)) [Waseem2020] and depends on build direction and raster ([Adibeig 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmbbm.2023.106025)) [Adibeig2023]. Given that the parallelogram blade stage is pre-loaded and cycled thousands of times per day, these results motivate keeping printed flexure stresses well below the fatigue reference curve and documenting the infill/orientation used. The linear thermal expansion coefficient of printed PLA has been measured by digital image correlation ([Botean 2018](https://doi.org/10.1051/e3sconf/20183201007)) [Botean2018; value not in abstract, typical literature range ≈60–90 ppm/K, UNVERIFIED]; the Leica guide independently notes that the first sections from a cold block run thick because of thermal expansion of the block itself [WEB_LEICA_MICROTOMY], so thermal drift of the printed frame is a second-order but real contributor to thickness error.

Dimensional accuracy sets what can be printed to fit and what must be machined or adjusted. Printed dimensions on a desktop printer differed significantly (p < 0.05) from nominal and varied with layer height, infill and orientation ([Melenka 2015](https://doi.org/10.1108/rpj-09-2013-0093)) [Melenka2015]; accuracy depends on nozzle diameter, temperature and speed ([Akbaş 2019](https://doi.org/10.1108/rpj-04-2019-0115)) [Akbas2019], and at 100% infill PLA+ was the most accurate and smoothest material tested ([Alsoufi 2018](https://doi.org/10.4236/msa.2018.91002)) [Alsoufi2018]. The commonly quoted ±0.1–0.2 mm tolerance is consistent with these studies but no single paper found states it as a general figure (UNVERIFIED as a quotable number); the FlexTome design should therefore not rely on printed features for any dimension that enters the 5 µm feed chain, the steel screw, the ratchet and the feeler-gauge strips carry that precision.

## Novelty positioning

What prior art covers:

- Low-cost and 3D-printed sectioning devices exist, but at ≥50 µm (vibrating razor, [Bazer1980]), <100 µm fresh/woody plant tissue with no feed mechanism ([Thomas2023]), and 250 µm–1 mm for organoids at ~US$853 ([MartinezMartin2024]). None embeds in paraffin, none uses a standard disposable microtome blade, and none reports a micrometer-resolution feed.
- The rocking-arm principle with screw feed and ratchet indexing is 19th-century prior art (Darwin 1885; [WEB_SCIENCEMUSEUM_ROCKER], [WEB_WHIPPLE_0683]) and was capable of EM-grade sections ([Eaves1954]); FlexTome cannot claim the kinematics as new.
- Cross-strip flexure pivots and their stiffness/centre-shift theory are fully established ([Wittrick1948], [Wittrick1951], [Haringx1949], [ZhaoBi2010], [Markovic2016]); differential screws are textbook ([SmithChetwynd2017]); FDM compliant stages reaching sub-micron motion are demonstrated ([Sharkey2016]).
- The access gap and the cost of commercial instruments are quantified ([Nelson2016], [Fleming2021], [Fleming2017], vendor prices).

What prior art does not cover, and FlexTome can honestly claim:

- A microtome of any kind, open-source, 3D-printed or otherwise, documented in the peer-reviewed literature with a bill of materials under US$100 that cuts paraffin sections in the 3–5 µm diagnostic range. The thinnest low-cost device found is an order of magnitude thicker (50 µm).
- The use of a cross-strip flexure pivot (rather than a journal, ball or knife-edge bearing) as the arm pivot of a rocking microtome, and a published analysis of its centre shift and stiffness in that role.
- A differential-screw feed indexed by a ratchet to 5 µm per stroke in a microtome built from standard metric fasteners; the Cambridge rocker used a single-pitch screw and the Minot design a micrometer screw.
- A monolithic printed parallelogram flexure stage carrying a standard low-profile disposable blade, with stiffness and natural frequency reported against the chatter literature ([Bayley1955], [Warshawsky1983]).
- Any measurement of cutting force in paraffin microtomy with absolute values; the only instrumented microtome found ([Willis1995]) reports relative variation. A load-cell measurement on FlexTome would fill a documented gap.
- Direct section-thickness measurement following the method of [Fujisawa2026] applied to a low-cost instrument, which would make the thickness claim stronger than that of most commercial-instrument descriptions.

Claims to avoid: that flexure pivots or differential screws are novel; that the rocker was abandoned because it could not cut thin sections (it could, [Eaves1954]); that any specific pathologist-density figure comes from the paywalled Lancet papers verbatim (cite [Nelson2016] for the numbers, the Series for the framing); and that cutting force in paraffin "is known to be" any specific value.
