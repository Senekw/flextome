# FlexTome: a 3D-printed, flexure-pivot rocking microtome with a differential-screw feed for 5 µm paraffin histology at a $60 bill of materials

**Category:** Engineering Mechanics / Biomedical Engineering (device design)
**Deliverables:** full parametric CAD (STEP assembly, 30-component STL set, interference-audited over the stroke), analytic and finite-element design analysis (stage and frame), Monte-Carlo section-thickness error budget, bill of materials, build-and-validation protocol.

---

## Abstract

Histopathology is the reference standard for cancer diagnosis, but much of sub-Saharan Africa has roughly one pathologist per million people and district hospitals lack the US$150,000–200,000 of equipment an essential pathology laboratory requires. The microtome that cuts 3–5 µm paraffin sections is one of the few items in that chain with no low-cost substitute: new manual rotary microtomes list at US$9,000–22,700, and the thinnest published open-source sectioning device cuts at ≥50 µm, an order of magnitude too thick for diagnostic H&E. We present FlexTome, a rocking-arm microtome designed to be printed on a 235 mm desktop FDM printer and completed with hardware-store fasteners. Three design decisions make 5 µm sectioning plausible from printed plastic: (i) the arm pivot is a cross-strip flexure built from three 0.20 mm spring-steel feeler-gauge strips, so the metrology loop contains no sliding or rolling joint and therefore no backlash; (ii) the 5 µm feed is generated by a differential screw (M8 × 1.25 against M8 × 1.0, 0.25 mm per revolution) indexed by a 50-tooth printed ratchet that the arm itself advances at the top of each stroke, so the micrometre-scale motion is carried entirely by steel threads while the printed parts only need millimetre tolerances; and (iii) the disposable blade rides on a monolithic printed parallelogram flexure stage preloaded against the screw. A crank-rocker drive gives a 54 mm arc stroke at ±12.5°. An analytic structural-loop model, calibrated by plane-stress finite-element models of the flexure stage and of the frame, predicts a feed-axis loop stiffness of 1.0 N/µm; the frame model caught a 0.83 µm/N tower compliance that the beam model had missed and drove a gusset revision that cut it to 0.21 µm/N. A Monte-Carlo budget combining thread-pitch error, printed ratchet error, cutting-force variation, pawl seating and thermal drift predicts a section thickness of 5.0 ± 0.6 µm (1 s.d.; 90 % interval 4.1–5.9 µm) at the 5 µm setting, comparable to the >±10 % deviation measured on commercial rotary microtomes in a 154-site survey. All structural resonances (≥357 Hz) lie far above the <10 Hz cutting excitation, so chatter is not predicted. The bill of materials is US$60.39, a >99 % reduction relative to new list price. The design, analysis code and a validation protocol are released so that the predictions can be tested on a physical build.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The problem
The cancer-diagnosis chain in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) fails at the laboratory before it fails at the clinic. A 2016 survey of African pathology resources found population-per-pathologist ratios from 84,000 (Mauritius) to 9.3 million (Niger), against 15,000–20,000 in the UK and US [Nelson 2016]; the Lancet Commission on diagnostics estimates that 47 % of the world's population has little or no access to diagnostics [Fleming 2021]; and an essential district-hospital pathology package costs US$150,000–200,000 in equipment [Fleming 2016]. Within that package, the microtome is the only instrument that converts a fixed, paraffin-embedded biopsy into the 3–5 µm section a pathologist can read. New manual rotary microtomes list at US$22,690 (Leica RM2125 RTS), US$21,548 (Sakura Accu-Cut SRM 200) and US$8,995 (Thermo HM 325); used units sell for US$2,100–7,950 (vendor prices, October 2026).

### 1.2 Why no low-cost microtome exists
Open-source hardware has reached the microscope: the OpenFlexure microscope is printed for US$20–200 and has been manufactured locally in Tanzania and Kenya [Collins 2020]. It has not reached the microtome. The published low-cost sectioning devices cut at ≥50 µm (a hair-clipper vibrating razor, [Bazer 1980]), <100 µm for hand-fed plant tissue (Rapid-Tome, [Thomas 2023]) and 250 µm–1 mm for live organoids (a US$853 HardwareX slicer, [Martinez-Martin 2024]). None embeds in paraffin, none uses a standard disposable microtome blade, and none has a micrometre-resolution feed. The barrier is mechanical: a 5 µm section demands that the knife-to-specimen distance repeat to better than ~1 µm stroke after stroke, while printed plastic parts have ±0.1–0.2 mm dimensional scatter [Melenka 2015], creep under sustained load [Waseem 2020], a Young's modulus 60 times lower than steel [Tymrak 2014], and sliding joints printed in plastic have backlash of tens of micrometres.

### 1.3 The design thesis
Our thesis is that these limitations can be designed around rather than overcome, using three principles from precision-instrument design:

1. **No sliding joints in the metrology loop.** Every joint between the blade edge and the specimen face that must repeat to <1 µm is a flexure (elastic) joint: the arm pivot is a cross-strip flexure, the blade stage is a parallelogram flexure, and the screw-to-stage interface is a preloaded nut. Flexures have zero backlash and no stick-slip [Smith 2000; Wittrick 1948].
2. **Keep the micrometre chain in steel.** The feed increment (5 µm) is set by the pitch difference of two standard steel threads and the tooth count of a ratchet; the printed parts are asked only to hold those steel parts in roughly the right place. The differential screw turns a 0.25 mm pitch difference into fine motion without a precision thread [Smith & Chetwynd 2017].
3. **Make the structural loop short and deep.** Section-to-section thickness variation equals loop compliance × cutting-force variation; chatter is a stiffness phenomenon [Bayley 1955; Warshawsky 1983]. The loop is a one-piece ribbed base and a gusseted tower whose root depth (60 mm) was set by finite-element analysis.

The rocking-arm kinematics themselves are not new: Horace Darwin's 1885 Cambridge rocker used a pivoted arm, screw feed and ratchet, and a modified rocker later cut sections for electron microscopy [Eaves 1954]. What is new is the realisation of that kinematic in printed plastic and commodity steel at a $60 bill of materials, with an explicit, FE-checked stiffness and error budget.

### 2.1 Architecture
Figure 1 shows the assembly. The coordinate frame has *x* along the feed (specimen → blade), *y* along the blade edge and *z* vertical.

### 2.2 Kinematics
The crank-rocker (Fig. 2a,b) gives θ = ±12.5°, a block-face stroke of 54 mm and a peak cutting speed of 174 mm/s at 60 rpm, one section per handwheel turn. Because the specimen travels on an arc, the face retracts 3.0 mm from the blade at the stroke ends and the cut face is a cylinder of radius 125 mm (sagitta 0.40 mm over a 20 mm block). The latter is the rocker's classical "curved section" property; because every stroke follows the identical arc, the curvature is cut into the block face once and the next section (the difference between two successive arcs) has uniform thickness. The effective clearance angle varies by ±4.6° across a 20 mm face; a 27° blade lean (clearance 9.5° at mid-face, rake 45.5° with a 35° blade facet) keeps it within 4.9–14.1°, inside the 3–15° range practitioners recommend (Fig. 2c).

![Figure 2](fig2_kinematics_pivot.png)

*Figure 2. Kinematics and pivot. (a) Face position and retraction vs crank angle. (b) Cutting speed. (c) Effective clearance angle across the face for three arm lengths. (d) Peak pivot-strip bending stress vs strip thickness; the chosen 0.20 mm × 20 mm strips reach 218 MPa at full swing, below the ~450 MPa endurance limit of hardened spring steel (feeler-gauge stock; the exact limit must be taken from the stock datasheet). Rotational stiffness is 0.267 N·m/rad (torque 0.058 N·m at full swing); the second-order centre shift is ≈0.1·L·θ² ≈ 95 µm at ±12.5°, which is repeatable and therefore shapes the arc rather than the thickness [Wittrick 1948; Haringx 1949].*

### 2.3 Feed resolution
With pitches p₁ = 1.25 mm and p₂ = 1.00 mm, one revolution advances the stage by p₁ − p₂ = 0.25 mm toward the specimen; one tooth of the 50-tooth ratchet is 5.0 µm. The selector shield exposes k = 1…6 teeth to the pawl, giving 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 or 30 µm settings (trimming at 20–30 µm, sectioning at 5 µm). Only the *difference* of the two threads matters, so ordinary rolled-thread hardware is sufficient: the budget assumes 1 % independent local pitch error on each thread and 3 µm peak-to-peak periodic error per revolution.

### 2.4 Blade stage design
Fig. 4a maps plate thickness and height against bending stress at the ±1 mm feed travel and against the x-stiffness that sets the preload. The chosen 1.0 × 44 mm plates give k_x = 4.9 N/mm (analytic) / 4.0 N/mm (FE), ≤5 N preload on the screw, 5.4 MPa peak plate stress and a creep safety factor of 2.8 against a 15 MPa sustained-stress limit for PLA [Waseem 2020]; PETG or ASA are drop-in alternatives. In the screw-held condition the stage is a short cantilever about the nut; the FE model (Fig. 5) gives 0.109 µm/N at the blade edge, half the analytic estimate, and this value is used in the budget.

A consequence of the differential principle that is easy to overlook: the screw body advances through the frame nut by the full 1.25 mm per revolution while the stage moves 0.25 mm, so over the ±1 mm stage range the screw, coupler, ratchet and knob translate ±5 mm. The ratchet is therefore a 14 mm drum rather than a wheel, the coupler sits in a 26 mm gap between the stage tab and the frame nut, and the thickness-selector shield is mounted on the stage body, not on the screw. A boolean interference audit of the assembly (11 arm angles over the full stroke, ±5 mm screw travel, all part pairs) found no unintended contacts; the only remaining intersections are the intended ones: pins in their bores, strips in their clamp pockets, the pawl tooth engaging the drum (16 mm³), and the striker bar meeting the pawl-rod flag between 6° and 12.5° of arm angle, which is the feed actuation itself (the rod is drawn at rest, so it registers as 50–170 mm³ of overlap in the static model).

### 2.5 Frame design: an FE-driven revision
The beam model treated the tower as a 70 × 70 mm box and the base as a plate rotating under the tower-root moment, and predicted that two M8 tie rods would triple base rigidity. A plane-stress FE model of the frame side-section (Fig. 6) showed otherwise: with the tower as actually drawn (32 mm deep), pivot-to-deck compliance was 0.83 µm/N, essentially all tower bending, and the tie rods changed it by only 2 % because the ribbed base was already stiff. Adding a forward gusset (the tower root widened to 60 mm over the first 55 mm of height, tapering to the 32 mm head, within the existing footprint and clear of the arm's swing) reduced the frame compliance to 0.21 µm/N. The tie rods were retained for a different reason: they pre-compress the printed base against creep and warp and allow the base to be printed in two halves on small printers.

## 3. Analysis methods

**Structural-loop model.** Each element between the blade edge and the specimen face was assigned a compliance along x under a unit cutting force: axial compression of the arm and pivot strips, the FE-calibrated frame (tower + base) compliance, the FE-calibrated stage compliance, screw column, screw-tip and thread contacts, blade overhang bending, and clamp compressions. PLA E = 3.5 GPa [Tymrak 2014], steel 200 GPa.

**Monte-Carlo thickness budget.** 20,000 strokes were simulated with: independent 1 % pitch errors on both threads and a 3 µm p-p periodic error; ratchet tooth-pitch error of 0.05 mm (1 s.d.) on a Ø60 mm printed wheel; stroke-to-stroke cutting-force variation of 0.4 N (1 s.d.) acting through the loop compliance; thermal drift over the 1 s stroke interval at 1 K per 30 min room drift; and 0.1 µm pawl seating. The nominal cutting force (2 N) and its variation are assumptions, no absolute paraffin cutting-force measurement exists in the literature [Willis 1995], and Fig. 4b shows the sensitivity.

**Finite elements.** 2-D plane-stress linear elasticity (scikit-fem, quadratic triangles) of the stage cross-section and of the frame side-section, with out-of-plane widths represented by scaling E per region. Verified against a cantilever benchmark (tip deflection 97.3 mm/N analytic vs 97.3 FE).

**Modal estimates.** Lumped single-degree-of-freedom estimates for arm bending in the swing plane, stage x-motion and whole-loop x-motion.

**Cost.** Printed-part cost from solid-modelled volume at US$20/kg PLA (an upper bound; ribbed and infilled prints weigh less); hardware at typical online/hardware-store prices.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Stiffness budget
Total feed-axis compliance is 1.00 µm/N (stiffness 1.00 N/µm). The three largest terms are blade overhang (0.25 µm/N), the frame (0.21, FE) and nut bedding in PLA (0.20); the pivot strips contribute only 0.02 (Fig. 3a). With the original slender tower the total would have been 1.62 µm/N.

![Figure 3](fig3_error_budget.png)

*Figure 3. (a) Compliance budget of the metrology loop. (b) Monte-Carlo section thickness at the 5 µm and 10 µm settings (n = 20,000). (c) 1-s.d. contribution of each error source at the 5 µm setting.*

### 4.2 Predicted section thickness
At the 5 µm setting the predicted section is **5.01 ± 0.56 µm** (1 s.d.), 90 % interval 4.09–5.92 µm; at 10 µm, 10.01 ± 0.79 µm. Cutting-force variation through the loop compliance (0.40 µm) and differential-thread pitch scatter (0.37 µm) dominate; printed-ratchet error (0.07 µm) and thermal drift within a ribbon (<0.01 µm) are negligible (Fig. 3c). With the slender tower the scatter would have been 0.76 µm (Fig. 4b). For context, direct measurement at 154 sites found more than two-thirds of sections from commercial rotary microtomes outside ±10 % of the dial setting [Fujisawa 2026]; FlexTome's predicted 1-s.d. scatter is ±11 %.

### 4.3 Thermal behaviour
The PLA loop expands 10.6 µm/K. This is irrelevant within a ribbon (strokes 1 s apart) but means the first sections after a temperature change or after mounting a new block will run thick or thin by several micrometres until trimmed, the same behaviour the Leica microtomy guide describes for a cold block, and handled by the same routine (trim, then section).

### 4.4 Dynamics and chatter
Estimated first modes: arm bending 357 Hz, whole-loop feed-axis 504 Hz, blade stage 1.7 kHz. A 20 mm face is cut in 118 ms, so the cutting excitation is concentrated below ~9 Hz; no resonance-driven chatter is predicted. The rotational mode of the arm on its pivot (≈4 Hz) is the actuated degree of freedom and is constrained by the connecting rod.

Against US$8,995–22,690 new and US$2,100–7,950 used, this is a >99 % and >97 % reduction respectively. The largest part (frame, 235 × 150 × 173 mm) fits an Ender-3-class 235 mm bed (or is split at the deck step for smaller printers); all other parts fit a 180 mm bed. No machining is required; the only cutting operations are shearing feeler-gauge strips to length and cutting threaded rod.

### 4.6 Cutting-force bound, improvement levers and the entry transient
Because no absolute paraffin cutting force has been published, the 2 ± 0.4 N assumption was bracketed with the pressing-and-slicing fracture model [Atkins 2004]: with a fracture work R = 20–80 J/m², wax yield stress 1–3 MPa, a 35° facet and friction coefficient 0.3, a 5 µm × 25 mm cut requires 0.6–2.4 N along the stroke and only 0.23–0.86 N along the feed axis (≤1.3 N for a dulled blade with doubled friction). The feed-axis force that drives thickness error is therefore likely below the 2 N assumed, and the 0.4 N (1 s.d.) variation is conservative; at 0.2 N the predicted scatter falls to 0.44 µm.

Of the two levers identified by the budget, reducing blade overhang from 2.5 to 2.0 mm is *not* available at the chosen geometry: the 1.0 mm pressure strip's top edge would then sit 0.09 mm inside the specimen's swept circle (it needs a 0.8 mm strip or a 29° lean). Seating the frame nut in a pressed brass insert instead of bare PLA is available and reduces loop compliance from 1.00 to 0.85 µm/N (scatter 0.56 → 0.52 µm).

Within a single section, the cutting force rises over the ~0.5 mm entry chamfer of the block face in ~3 ms. Driving the 504 Hz, 3 %-damped loop with that ramp gives an 18 % dynamic overshoot (0.36 µm on a 2 µm quasi-static deflection) that rings down within 42 ms of the 118 ms cut, bounding within-section thickness non-uniformity from this source to ≈0.4 µm over the first 7 mm of the face.

## 5. Discussion

**What the analysis establishes.** The predicted 5.0 ± 0.6 µm is a model result, not a measurement. Its value is that it identifies, quantitatively, what limits a printed microtome: not feed resolution (the steel differential screw delivers 5 µm trivially) but loop stiffness against cutting-force variation, and within that, blade overhang, tower bending and nut bedding. The frame FE is the clearest lesson of the study: a beam-theory budget built on an idealised tower section under-predicted tower compliance eight-fold and credited the tie rods with a stiffening they do not provide; the FE both exposed the error and pointed to the fix (a gusset within the footprint). The next lever is seating the frame nut in a brass insert (Section 4.6); reducing blade overhang below 2.5 mm requires a thinner pressure strip or a steeper lean.

**Relation to prior art.** The Darwin rocker already combined arm, screw and ratchet; what it lacked was a bearing that a printed machine could reproduce. Replacing its hardened steel knife-edge or journal pivot with crossed feeler-gauge strips removes the one component that required a machine shop. The differential screw is a textbook element [Smith & Chetwynd 2017] but has not previously been reported in a microtome, where it allows the 5 µm increment to come from two hardware-store threads rather than a micrometer spindle. The parallelogram blade stage follows the OpenFlexure precedent that FDM compliant mechanisms achieve micron-scale repeatability [Sharkey 2016].

**Limitations and risks.**
1. *Cutting force is assumed.* The 2 ± 0.4 N figure is an engineering estimate; no absolute value exists in the literature. The sensitivity curve (Fig. 4b) shows that doubling the variation to 0.8 N raises scatter to 1.04 µm. A load-cell measurement on the build is therefore both a validation step and a contribution in its own right.
2. *No specimen retraction on the return stroke.* The face passes the edge on the upstroke flush with it. Basic manual rotary microtomes without retraction are used routinely, but knife lines are possible; a 50–100 µm retraction cam acting on the stage is a planned addition.
3. *Cylindrical block face.* The 0.40 mm sagitta over 20 mm is the rocker's known property; sections flatten on the water bath. Block height is limited to ~25 mm by the clearance-angle variation.
4. *PLA creep and humidity.* The stage flexure is loaded only while displaced from neutral (≤5 N, 5.4 MPa); the user returns the screw to centre after each block. PETG/ASA or 100 % infill at 0.1 mm layers reduce creep further [Waseem 2020].
5. *Thread backlash.* The flexure preload (≤10 N) and the unidirectional cutting load keep the nut flanks engaged; reversing the feed requires re-trimming, as on any manual microtome.
6. *Blade clamping.* Two M4 grub screws from behind against a 1 mm steel pressure strip is simpler than a commercial clamp; blade seating repeatability must be verified.

**Broader impact.** A $60 microtome does not create a pathology service; it needs embedding, staining, a microscope and, above all, a pathologist or a telepathology link. But it removes the single highest-cost mechanical item from the chain, and does so with a device that a hospital workshop or a school with a desktop printer can build and repair. Combined with the OpenFlexure microscope, it brings the entire physical chain from block to image under US$300.

## 6. Validation plan (for the physical build)

1. **Print and assemble** (frame 100 % infill in the pivot and deck regions, 0.2 mm layers; stage flexure 100 % infill, 0.1 mm layers, plates printed vertically). Record print mass and time.
2. **Static loop stiffness.** Apply 0.5–3 N to the block face along x with a hanging mass over a pulley; read displacement with a 1 µm dial indicator (or a USB microscope on a scale) at the blade edge. Target ≥0.8 N/µm. Compare to the budget element by element by moving the indicator.
3. **Feed calibration.** Advance 50 teeth (one revolution) and measure 250 ± 5 µm stage travel with the indicator; repeat 10× for pitch-error scatter.
4. **Section thickness.** (a) Block-consumption method: dial indicator on the trimmed face before and after 100 sections at 5 µm → mean thickness. (b) Gravimetric: weigh ribbons of 20 sections of known face area (paraffin density 0.90 g/cm³). (c) Optical: measure 20 individual sections with a confocal/interference microscope if available, following [Fujisawa 2026]. Report mean, s.d. and the fraction within ±10 %.
5. **Cutting force.** Mount a 5 N load cell under the stage foot; record force vs crank angle over 50 strokes to replace the assumed 2 ± 0.4 N.
6. **Section quality.** Cut 5 µm ribbons from standard test blocks (liver, kidney, skin) on FlexTome and a commercial microtome; H&E stain; blinded scoring (compression, chatter, knife lines, folds) by two histotechnologists.
7. **Durability.** 10,000 strokes (≈3 h at 60 rpm, motor-driven), then repeat steps 2–4.

---

## 7. Conclusion

A rocking microtome whose precision-critical joints are flexures and whose micrometre feed chain is entirely steel can be built from printed PLA and hardware-store parts for US$60. The FE-checked design analysis predicts 5.0 ± 0.6 µm sections at a loop stiffness of 1.0 N/µm, with stiffness, not feed resolution, as the limiting quantity, and identifies blade overhang, tower bending and nut seating as the levers for improvement. The complete CAD, analysis code and validation protocol are released with this report so that the prediction can be tested, and the design iterated, by anyone with a desktop printer.
